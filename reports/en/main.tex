\begin{abstract}
We give a structural proof that every exact bilinear algorithm for $3\times3$
matrix multiplication over the two-element field $\FF$ requires at least
21 scalar multiplications. The argument combines certified finite premises
with a short algebraic obstruction. Quotient occupation bounds, including
eight strengthened two-dimensional bounds certified by CNF/DRAT proofs,
force the high-rank first factors of a hypothetical 20-term decomposition
into an affine row or column coset. Finite geometry then forces sixteen
rank-one, one rank-two, and three rank-three first factors: the
matrix-rank profile $(16,1,3)$, exactly saturating a $27\times27$ split
flattening. Equality yields orthogonal-idempotent identities; a
tensor-specific cross-factor product identity makes two invertible first
factors impossible, contradicting the required three.

Qiushi Engine independently developed the argument through sustained,
end-to-end autonomous mathematical research, initially addressing rank-22
construction and rank-23 optimality.
Numerical experiments clarified exactness, quotient searches exposed the
gap between supports and complete decompositions, and a return to the
full tensor revealed the equality case that resolves the lower-bound
argument. A documented research trajectory, reconstructed from Qiushi
Engine's research memory, connects the evolving questions, experiments,
corrections, and mathematical insights that led to the proof.
Together with the accompanying notes, programs, results, and proof
certificates, it makes both the argument and the research process
available for examination and reuse. The result leaves
$21\le R_{\FF}(\Tmmm)\le23$ and constrains zero-excess rank-22
decompositions, whose existence remains open.
\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords}\quad
Matrix multiplication; tensor rank; bilinear complexity; finite fields;
computer-assisted proof; autonomous mathematical research
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ordinary multiplication of two $3\times3$ matrices uses 27 scalar
multiplications. A bilinear algorithm reduces this count by multiplying selected
linear combinations of entries from the two inputs, then combining the
results. Constructing one such algorithm establishes an upper bound;
proving a lower bound requires ruling out every shorter algorithm in the
same model. The field matters: throughout this report the scalar arithmetic
is over the two-element field, where addition is modulo two.

The count measures algebraic complexity, not machine instructions or
hardware throughput. The scientific question is which exact decompositions
are possible. Our proof identifies a situation in which apparently separate
constraints on the factors become an equality, and that equality imposes
identities strong enough to rule out the proposed decomposition.

The \emph{bilinear complexity} $R(T)$ of a tensor $T$ over a field $k$ is the minimum number of scalar multiplications in any bilinear algorithm that computes the multilinear map represented by $T$.  Equivalently, it is the tensor rank of~$T$: the minimum $r$ such that $T$ can be written as a sum of $r$ simple (rank-one) tensors. For the $3\times3$ matrix multiplication tensor~$\Tmmm$ over~$\FF$, the earlier baseline inherited by this research was
\[
  20\;\le\;R_{\FF}(\Tmmm)\;\le\;23.
\]
The upper bound~23 is achieved by Laderman's algorithm~\cite{laderman1976noncommutative}, adapted to~$\FF$, and by explicit 23-multiplication schemes found by SAT search~\cite{heule2019waysa}.  The lower bound~20 was proved by Wang~\cite{wangautomated} using an automated framework that combines orbit classification of linear restrictions on the first matrix factor with dynamic programming over four lower-bound techniques (flattening, forced product, degenerate reduction, and substitution with backtracking).  Wang's method produces a machine-checkable proof certificate with~496 representative subspaces; for the certificate used here, we re-ran Wang's verifier on the pinned file and independently re-expanded those 496 nodes to the complete lower-bound table with zero value mismatches.

General lower-bound methods for matrix multiplication, including Bläser's quadratic rank bounds and Landsberg's asymptotic rank improvements~\cite{blaser1999focs,landsberg2012lower}, leave exact small tensors such as $\Tmmm$ unresolved. Over~$\FF$, the decomposition geometry differs from that over larger fields because the two-element field has no nontrivial scalar multiples.

\begin{theorem}[Main result]\label{thm:main}
  $R_{\FF}(\Tmmm)\ge 21$.  That is, every bilinear algorithm for $3\times 3$ matrix multiplication over~$\FF$ requires at least~21 scalar multiplications.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\textbf{The mechanism.}\quad
The proof excludes length-20 decompositions by the interplay of two constraints that are individually insufficient but complementary at length~20.  Wang's quotient occupation bounds restrict how many factors of a decomposition can belong to any subspace of the factor space.  The split-flattening inequality forces the sum of ranks of all factors to be at least~27.  The length-20 caps used by the proof are exactly tight in three places: a line with $L=19$ has cap~1, an all-high two-plane after our raise to $L=19$ has cap~1, and an affine hyperplane in the normalized coset with $L=17$ has cap~3.  These three cap families force the rank sum to equal exactly~27---a zero-excess endpoint.  At zero excess the split-flattened terms form a direct-sum decomposition of a permutation matrix, yielding orthogonal-idempotent identities that constrain the algebraic dependence between the matrix factors.  For the matrix-multiplication tensor, these identities make a pair of invertible first-slot factors impossible, while the forced rank profile demands three such factors.

\noindent\textbf{Consequences and further questions.}\quad
The theorem leaves the exact binary-field rank in $[21,23]$. The same
saturation identity restricts rank-22 decompositions whose first-factor
rank sum is exactly~27: at most one of those factors may be invertible.
Section~\ref{sec:outlook} derives this consequence and explains why the
current occupation bounds do not force equality at length~21.

\noindent\textbf{Relation to prior work.}\quad
The flattening rank-sum constraint $\sum_t\rk(A_t)\ge\rk(\Phi_A(T))$ follows from subadditivity of matrix rank; flattenings are standard tools in algebraic complexity~\cite{burgisser1997algebraic,blaser1999focs}. The structure theorem that matrices with pairwise rank-one differences lie in a common affine row or column coset belongs to Hua's geometry of matrices~\cite{hua1945geometries,wan1996geometry,pazzis2015hua}. Rank-additive decompositions and idempotent identities are classical linear algebra; related identities appear in~\cite{cochran1934distribution,tian2001rank}, and Lemma~\ref{lem:saturation} is proved directly below over the field needed here. The specific route presented here combines CNF/DRAT certification of eight quotient bounds with rank-additivity at zero excess and the tensor-specific product identity~\eqref{eq:product}, obtaining a structural contradiction rather than a final enumeration of first-factor profiles.

\section{Setting and Wang's Certificate}\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Tensor Convention}

The $3\times 3$ matrix multiplication tensor $\Tmmm$ lives in $(\FF^9)^{\otimes 3}$, where each copy of~$\FF^9$ is identified with the space of $3\times 3$ matrices over~$\FF$.  We index it by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tensor}
  T[3i+j,\;3j+k,\;3i+k] = 1 \qquad (0\le i,j,k<3),
\end{equation}
with all other entries zero. Under the coordinate pairing
$\langle A,X\rangle=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}X_{ij}=\tr(A^TX)$, its trilinear
form on input and test matrices is $(X,Y,Z)\mapsto\tr(XYZ^T)$.
A rank-one summand is written $A_t\otimes B_t\otimes C_t$, where
$A_t,B_t,C_t$ are coefficient matrices. An exact decomposition of length~$r$ is an identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-decomposition}
  \Tmmm \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^r A_t\otimes B_t\otimes C_t,
\end{equation}
which computes $XY=\sum_t\langle A_t,X\rangle\langle B_t,Y\rangle C_t$
using $r$ scalar multiplications and proves $R(\Tmmm)\le r$; equality
requires a separate lower bound.

For $W\le\FF^9$, let $q_W:\FF^9\to\FF^9/W$ and define
$T_W=(q_W\otimes\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id})\Tmmm$.
Its dual first-input space is $W^\perp$ under the coordinate pairing:
quotienting coefficient factors is equivalent to restricting the first
input to this annihilator, not to the subspace~$W$ itself.

The tensor has a symmetry relevant to the proof: the map $(A,B,C)\mapsto(A^T,C,B)$ preserves the trilinear form because $\tr(ABC^T)=\tr(A^TCB^T)$.  This transposition symmetry allows us to reduce the analysis of ``row-type'' decompositions to ``column-type'' by transposing the $A$-factors.

\subsection{Wang's Quotient Occupation Inequality}

Wang~\cite{wangautomated} proves $R_{\FF}(\Tmmm)\ge20$ by a certified computation. Write $L_0(W)$ for the lower bound in the fixed, inherited table, so $L_0(W)\le R(T_W)$. The table value is not the true quotient rank and is not redefined when we prove a stronger bound. The fundamental inequality is:

\begin{proposition}[Occupation inequality]\label{prop:occupation}
  Let $\Tmmm=\sum_{t=1}^n A_t\otimes B_t\otimes C_t$ be a length-$n$ decomposition, and let $W\le\FF^9$.  If $m(W)$ denotes the number of indices~$t$ with $A_t\in W$, then
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:occ}
    m(W)\;\le\; n-R(T_W)\;\le\;n-L_0(W).
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Terms with $A_t\in W$ vanish under $q_W$. The remaining terms give a decomposition of $T_W$ of length at most $n-m(W)$; hence $R(T_W)\le n-m(W)$.
\end{proof}

Wang's certificate contains 496~representative subspaces.  The full lookup table (LUT) is obtained by expanding these representatives under the tensor symmetry group---the action of $\GL_3(\FF)\times\GL_3(\FF)$ on the $A$-slot by $(P,Q)\cdot A = P^TAQ^{-T}$, together with the transpose $(A,B,C)\mapsto(A^T,C,B)$.  The expanded LUT has exactly $8{,}283{,}458$ entries, one for each subspace of~$\FF^9$, which matches the sum of Gaussian binomial coefficients $\sum_{d=0}^9\binom{9}{d}_2$.  We independently re-expanded the full table from the 496 certificate nodes using a BFS orbit closure with verified tensor-preserving generators, and obtained zero value mismatches and zero missing subspaces.

For invertible $U,V,H$, the complete coefficient action is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-action}
 (A,B,C)\longmapsto
 (U^TAV^{-T},\;V^TBH^{-T},\;U^{-1}CH).
\end{equation}
It is the pullback of $(X,Y,Z)\mapsto(UXV^{-1},VYH^{-1},U^{-T}ZH^T)$,
which preserves $\tr(XYZ^T)$. Thus the first-slot actions above extend
to genuine symmetries of the full tensor, preserving quotient ranks.

\subsection{Finite Premises and Their Sources}

The proof uses the inherited table and separately certified improvements:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item\label{it:lines} \textbf{All 511 lines have $L=19$.}\quad For every one-dimensional subspace $\langle a\rangle\le\FF^9$, the table gives $L_0(\langle a\rangle)=19$.

  \item\label{it:dim2} \textbf{Eight dimension-two orbits satisfy $R(T_W)\ge19$.}\quad Among the $43{,}435$ two-dimensional subspaces of~$\FF^9$, the symmetry group partitions them into 14 orbits. Exactly 8 orbits, indexed 484--491 in the pinned certificate, consist of planes whose three nonzero matrices all have rank~$\ge2$. They contain $32{,}018$ subspaces. The inherited value is $L_0(W_i)=18$ for each representative. Section~\ref{sec:dim2} proves $R(T_{W_i})\ge19$ by infeasibility of its target-18 occupation system. Tensor symmetries give $T_{W_i}\cong T_{gW_i}$, transferring the improved bound throughout each orbit. These indices refer to the pinned file, not to arbitrary versions of the upstream table.

  \item\label{it:coset} \textbf{Coset hyperplane values.}\quad After normalization (see Section~\ref{sec:coset}), the relevant four-dimensional coset span $S$ has $L_0(S)=14$.  Its 15 three-dimensional hyperplanes split into one rank-one family with $L=15$ and fourteen affine-plane hyperplanes each with $L=17$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proof of the Main Theorem}\label{sec:proof}

The verified bound $R_{\FF}(\Tmmm)\ge20$ already excludes shorter decompositions. It therefore suffices to exclude length~20. Assume for contradiction that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
  \Tmmm \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{20} A_t\otimes B_t\otimes C_t
\end{equation}
is an exact decomposition over~$\FF$, with every summand nonzero.

\subsection{Split Flattening and the Rank Budget}\label{sec:flattening}

The \emph{$A$-split flattening} of a tensor $T\in(\FF^9)^{\otimes 3}$ is the linear map $\Phi_A(T):\FF^{27}\to\FF^{27}$ obtained by splitting each $A$-coordinate into its row and column indices.  Concretely, the matrix $P=\Phi_A(\Tmmm)$ has entry $P[(i,b),(j,c)]=T[3i+j,b,c]$, where $(i,b)$ and $(j,c)$ are composite indices with $0\le i,j<3$ and $0\le b,c<9$.  From the indexing~\eqref{eq:tensor}, one checks that $P$ is a $27\times27$ permutation matrix: each row and column has exactly one nonzero entry.  In particular, $\rk(P)=27$.

For each summand, the split-flattened term $M_t=\Phi_A(A_t\otimes B_t\otimes C_t)$ is the Kronecker product $A_t\otimes\mathbf{b}_t\mathbf{c}_t^T$, where $\mathbf{b}_t=\operatorname{vec}(B_t)$ and $\mathbf{c}_t=\operatorname{vec}(C_t)$.  Since $B_t\ne 0$ and $C_t\ne 0$, the outer product $\mathbf{b}_t\mathbf{c}_t^T$ has rank one, and therefore
\[
  \rk(M_t) = \rk(A_t).
\]
By subadditivity of rank,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rksum}
  27 = \rk(P) = \rk\!\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{20}M_t\Bigr) \;\le\; \sum_{t=1}^{20}\rk(A_t).
\end{equation}
Writing $e_t=\rk(A_t)-1\in\{0,1,2\}$ for each factor, we obtain $\sum_{t=1}^{20}e_t\ge 7$.  Since each term contributes at most~2, at least four factors have $e_t\ge 1$, hence $\rk(A_t)\ge 2$.  We call these factors \emph{high-rank}.

\subsection{Distinctness of First Factors}

By value~\ref{it:lines}, every line satisfies $L_0(\langle a\rangle)=19$. The occupation inequality gives $m(\langle a\rangle)\le1$. Over~$\FF$, a nonzero matrix has no nontrivial scalar multiple, so at most one factor equals~$a$. Thus the twenty $A$-factors are pairwise distinct.

\subsection{Eight Two-Dimensional Quotient Bounds}\label{sec:dim2}

Let $A_s,A_t$ be distinct high-rank factors and set $W=\langle A_s,A_t\rangle$. Since $m(W)\ge2$, equation~\eqref{eq:occ} gives $R(T_W)\le18$.

Now suppose the third nonzero element $A_s+A_t$ also has rank~$\ge 2$.  Then $W$ is an ``all-high'' plane: all three of its nonzero elements have matrix rank at least~2.  The independent orbit classification (Section~\ref{sec:setting}) shows that such planes fall into exactly eight orbits, with certificate indices 484--491.  We claim:

\begin{proposition}[Dimension-two raises]\label{prop:dim2}
  For each orbit representative $W_i$ ($i\in\{484,\ldots,491\}$), the occupation system
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:occ-system}
    x_q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0},\quad \sum_{q\in(\FF^9\!/W)\setminus 0}x_q = 18,\quad
    \sum_{q\in (V/W)\setminus 0}x_q \le 18-L_0(V) \;\;\text{for every nonzero proper}\;V/W,
  \end{equation}
  has no integer solution, where $W=W_i$ and $V$ ranges over its strict superspaces below $\FF^9$. Consequently, $R(T_{W_i})\ge19$.
\end{proposition}

If $R(T_{W_i})\le18$, choose a nonzero-term quotient decomposition of length $n_0\le18$. Its first-factor multiplicities have total $n_0$ and satisfy every cap $n_0-L_0(V)$, by quotienting again by $V/W_i$. Adding $18-n_0$ to any one multiplicity preserves every enlarged cap $18-L_0(V)$ and gives total~18. Thus a short decomposition necessarily yields an integer solution of~\eqref{eq:occ-system}. The converse is not claimed: occupation counts do not supply compatible second and third factors.

Each system has 127 multiplicity variables and $29{,}210$ subspace constraints, with caps $18-L_0(V)$. SAT encoding uses Boolean copies and sequential-counter constraints; CaDiCaL returns UNSAT and \texttt{drat-trim} verifies the proofs. Multiplicities are essential for orbit~489, where one direction has cap~2. Separate checks find feasible occupation vectors at target~19 and no row violations for projected rank-23 decompositions. These are calibration checks, not rank-19 constructions or proofs of the encoding's soundness.

\medskip

With $R(T_W)\ge19$ established for all-high planes, the occupation inequality gives $m(W)\le1$, contradicting $m(W)\ge2$. Therefore $A_s+A_t$ must have rank~$1$ whenever $A_s,A_t$ are both high-rank, and we conclude:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rkone}
  \rk(A_s-A_t) = \rk(A_s+A_t) = 1 \qquad\text{for every pair of high-rank factors}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Affine Coset Containment}\label{sec:coset}

The structure of families of matrices with pairwise rank-one differences is classical.  The following is a consequence of the adjacency theory in Hua's geometry of matrices~\cite{hua1945geometries,wan1996geometry,pazzis2015hua}:

\begin{lemma}[Coset structure]\label{lem:coset}
  Let $H\subset M_{3\times 3}(\FF)$ be a set of at least four distinct matrices such that $\rk(a-b)=1$ for every $a\ne b$ in $H$.  Then $H$ is contained in an affine column coset $p+u\cdot(\FF^3)^T$ or an affine row coset $p+\FF^3\cdot v^T$, for some $p\in M_{3\times 3}(\FF)$ and nonzero $u,v\in\FF^3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Fix $a,b,c\in H$ distinct and write $a+b=u_1v_1^T$, $a+c=u_2v_2^T$.  Then $b+c=u_1v_1^T+u_2v_2^T$ has rank one, which requires either $u_1=u_2$ (column type) or $v_1=v_2$ (row type) over~$\FF$.  The type is unique: if both held, then $b+c=0$, contradicting $\rk(b+c)=1$.  For a fourth point $d\in H$, write $a+d=u'(v')^T$.  If $a,b,c$ are column type with common left vector~$u$, then $b+d$ and $c+d$ both have rank one.  Since $b+d=(u_1v_1^T)+(u'(v')^T)$ and $c+d=(u_2v_2^T)+(u'(v')^T)$, rank one forces $u'=u$ unless $(v')^T$ simultaneously equals both~$v_1^T$ and~$v_2^T$; but $v_1\ne v_2$ (since $b\ne c$), so $u'=u$.  Thus $d$ lies in $a+u\cdot(\FF^3)^T$.
\end{proof}

Let $H\subset\{A_1,\ldots,A_{20}\}$ denote the set of high-rank factors.  By~\eqref{eq:rkone} and Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} (with $|H|\ge 4$), all high-rank factors lie in a common affine coset of column type or row type.

The tensor symmetry $(A,B,C)\mapsto(A^T,C,B)$ transforms a row-type coset $p+\FF^3\cdot v^T$ into the column-type coset $p^T+v\cdot(\FF^3)^T$.  It suffices to treat column type.

\subsection{The Forced Rank Profile}

Normalize the column coset using $\GL_3(\FF)\times\GL_3(\FF)$, which acts on the $A$-slot by $(P,Q)\cdot A = P^TAQ^{-T}$.  Since $\GL_3(\FF)$ acts transitively on $\FF^3\setminus\{0\}$, left multiplication sends $u$ to~$e_1$.  The coset is then $p+e_1\cdot(\FF^3)^T$, where $p$ is a fixed base matrix.  Translating by a coset element makes the first row of~$p$ zero.  The eight coset elements $\{p+e_1w^T:w\in\FF^3\}$ differ only in their first row.  Let $D$ be the lower $2\times 3$ block of~$p$.  In the rank-two case below, the stabilizer of the column direction~$e_1$ acts transitively on full-rank $2\times3$ lower blocks up to row and column base change, so we may take $D=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, equivalently $p=272=\operatorname{diag}(0,1,1)$ in the integer encoding of Appendix~\ref{app:data}.  The values $L_0(S)=14$, $L_0(R)=15$, and the fourteen $L=17$ hyperplanes used below refer to this normalized span~$S=\langle272,4,2,1\rangle$; $\GL_3\times\GL_3$ invariance transfers them to every rank-two column coset.

Explicitly, left multiplication by $\operatorname{diag}(1,K)$ fixes
$e_1$ and induces any $K\in\GL_2(\FF)$ on the lower block.
For rank-two $D$, choose a right action $G\in\GL_3(\FF)$ sending its
row space to $\langle e_2^T,e_3^T\rangle$, then choose $K$ to put its
two row vectors in that order; thus $KDG$ is the stated standard block.
Both actions belong to the tensor symmetries described in
Section~\ref{sec:setting}.

If $\rk(D)=0$, every coset element has rank at most~1, so no high-rank factors can lie in the coset, contradicting $H\ne\emptyset$.  If $\rk(D)=1$, the row span of~$D$ is one-dimensional, and a coset element has rank~2 exactly when its first row lies outside this span; over~$\FF$, this gives $8-2=6$ rank-2 elements and 2 rank-1 elements, with no rank-3 elements.  The total excess from at most 6 high-rank factors would then be $\le 6\cdot 1=6<7$, contradicting~\eqref{eq:rksum}.  So $\rk(D)=2$.

With $\rk(D)=2$, the coset has four rank-2 elements (first row in the 2-dimensional row span of~$D$, but not making rank~3) and four rank-3 elements (first row outside the row span of~$D$).  The span of the coset is the four-dimensional subspace
\[
  S = \langle p,\; e_1e_1^T,\; e_1e_2^T,\; e_1e_3^T\rangle.
\]
By value~\ref{it:coset}, $L_0(S)=14$.  The 15 three-dimensional hyperplanes of~$S$ comprise:
\begin{itemize}
  \item One hyperplane $R=\langle e_1e_1^T,e_1e_2^T,e_1e_3^T\rangle$ whose nonzero elements have rank one, with $L_0(R)=15$.
  \item Fourteen hyperplanes corresponding to the fourteen affine planes in $(\FF^3)^T\cong\FF^3$, each with $L=17$.
\end{itemize}
Every set of four coset labels (first-row vectors) that forms an affine plane of~$\AG(3,2)$ lies in one of these fourteen hyperplanes.

The fourteen bounds~17 are essential below; the values $L_0(S)=14$ and $L_0(R)=15$ are auxiliary normalization checks.

Each affine-plane hyperplane $H_\pi$ has $L_0(H_\pi)=17$, so by~\eqref{eq:occ}, at most $20-17=3$ of the 20 factors can have their $A$-factor in~$H_\pi$.

\begin{lemma}[Cap bound]\label{lem:cap}
  Every 5-element subset of $\FF^3$ contains a 4-point affine plane.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Among five points $p_1,\ldots,p_5\in\FF^3$ there are $\binom{5}{2}=10$ unordered pair sums $p_i+p_j$, but only $|\FF^3\setminus\{0\}|=7$ possible nonzero values.  Hence two distinct pairs share the same sum: $p_i+p_j=p_k+p_\ell$.  The pairs cannot share exactly one endpoint (that would give $p_j=p_k$ or $p_j=p_\ell$, merging two of our five points), so they are disjoint.  Their four endpoints satisfy $p_i+p_j+p_k+p_\ell=0$, forming a four-point affine plane.
\end{proof}

The 14 four-point affine planes in $\AG(3,2)$ each contribute a constraint $\le 3$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:cap} shows that any 5 points contain such a plane.  Therefore:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:highcount}
  |H|\le 4.
\end{equation}
Since Section~\ref{sec:flattening} established $|H|\ge 4$, we have $|H|=4$.

The four rank-3 labels form an affine plane of $\AG(3,2)$ (the complement of the row span of~$D$ in~$\FF^3$), so at most 3 of the 4 high-rank factors can be rank~3.  With $|H|=4$ and $h_3\le 3$, the rank excess is
\[
  \sum_{t=1}^{20}(\rk(A_t)-1) = 16\cdot 0 + (4-h_3)\cdot 1 + h_3\cdot 2 = 4+h_3.
\]
This must be $\ge 7$, hence $h_3\ge 3$.  Combined with $h_3\le 3$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profile}
  h_2=1,\quad h_3=3,\quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t=1}^{20}\rk(A_t) = 16+2+3+3+3 = 27.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Saturation and the Algebraic Contradiction}\label{sec:saturation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:profile} says that equality holds in the rank inequality~\eqref{eq:rksum}.  This has a strong algebraic consequence.

\begin{lemma}[Rank-additive decomposition]\label{lem:saturation}
  Let $P$ be an invertible $n\times n$ matrix over a field~$k$, and let $P=\sum_{t=1}^r M_t$ with $\sum_{t=1}^r\rk(M_t)=\rk(P)=n$.  Then
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:idempotent}
    M_t P^{-1} M_s \;=\; \delta_{ts}\,M_t \qquad\text{for all}\;s,t.
  \end{equation}
  In particular, the $\{E_t=M_tP^{-1}\}$ form a complete system of orthogonal idempotents: $E_t^2=E_t$, $E_tE_s=0$ for $t\ne s$, and $\sum_tE_t=I$. Here orthogonality means pairwise annihilation, not an inner-product condition.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Since $\sum_t\rk(M_t)=n=\rk(P)$, the column spaces $\operatorname{col}(M_t)$ form a direct sum equal to~$k^n$, and similarly for the row spaces.  For any $x$, write $Pz=M_sx$ for $z=P^{-1}M_sx$.  Then $\sum_t M_tz=M_sx$, and by uniqueness of the direct-sum decomposition, $M_tz=0$ for $t\ne s$ and $M_sz=M_sx$.
\end{proof}

This is an elementary rank-additivity lemma; related idempotent rank identities are classical~\cite{cochran1934distribution,tian2001rank}.

Now we exploit the specific structure of the matrix-multiplication tensor.  The split-flattened term $M_t=M(A_t,B_t,C_t)$ has rows and columns indexed by pairs $(i,b)$ and $(j,c)$ with $0\le i,j<3$ and $0\le b,c<9$.  We compute the product explicitly.

\begin{proposition}[Product identity]\label{prop:product}
  For the tensor~$\Tmmm$ with convention~\eqref{eq:tensor},
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:product}
    M(A_t,B_t,C_t)\;P^{-1}\;M(A_s,B_s,C_s)
    \;=\;
    M\bigl(A_t B_s C_t^T A_s,\;\; B_t,\;\; C_s\bigr).
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Index rows and columns by pairs $(i,b)$ and $(j,c)$ with $0\le i,j<3$ and $0\le b,c<9$.  The term $M_t$ has entries $M_t[(i,b),(j,c)] = A_t[i,j]\cdot\mathbf{b}_t[b]\cdot\mathbf{c}_t[c]$, where $\mathbf{b}_t=\operatorname{vec}(B_t)$ and $\mathbf{c}_t=\operatorname{vec}(C_t)$.  Since $P$ is a permutation matrix over~$\FF$, $P^{-1}=P^T$, so $P^{-1}[(k,d),(m,e)]=T[3m+k,e,d]$.

  The $((i,b),(j,c))$-entry of the left side factors as $\mathbf{b}_t[b]\cdot\mathbf{c}_s[c]$ times
  \[
    \sum_{k,m} A_t[i,k]\,A_s[m,j] \;\underbrace{\sum_{d,e}\mathbf{c}_t[d]\,T[3m+k,e,d]\,\mathbf{b}_s[e]}_{(\star)}.
  \]
  For fixed $m,k$, the inner sum $(\star)$ is the coefficient of $X[m,k]$ in $\tr(XB_sC_t^T)$, namely $(B_sC_t^T)[k,m]$. Explicitly, the nonzero tensor entries have $e=3k+k'$, $d=3m+k'$ for $k'\in\{0,1,2\}$, giving $(\star)=\sum_{k'}C_t[m,k']\,B_s[k,k']$. Summing over $k,m$:
  \[
    \sum_{k,m}A_t[i,k]\,(B_sC_t^T)[k,m]\,A_s[m,j] = (A_tB_sC_t^TA_s)[i,j].
  \]
  Thus the full entry equals $M(A_tB_sC_t^TA_s,B_t,C_s)[(i,b),(j,c)]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Completion of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
  By~\eqref{eq:profile}, three of the $A$-factors---say $A_1,A_2,A_3$---are invertible ($\rk=3$).  Take $t=s$ in~\eqref{eq:idempotent} and~\eqref{eq:product}:
  \[
    M(A_tB_tC_t^TA_t,\,B_t,\,C_t) = M(A_t,\,B_t,\,C_t).
  \]
  Since $B_t\ne 0$ and $C_t\ne 0$, the map $X\mapsto M(X,B_t,C_t)$ is injective on $3\times 3$ matrices (it is a Kronecker product with a nonzero outer factor).  Therefore $A_tB_tC_t^TA_t=A_t$, which gives
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:diagonal}
    B_tC_t^T = A_t^{-1},
  \end{equation}
  so $B_t$ and $C_t$ are invertible.

  Now take $t\ne s$ with both $A_t,A_s$ invertible (say $t=1,s=2$).  The off-diagonal identity~\eqref{eq:idempotent} and~\eqref{eq:product} give
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:off-diagonal-zero}
    M(A_1B_2C_1^TA_2,\,B_1,\,C_2) = 0.
  \end{equation}
  Since $B_1\ne 0$ and $C_2\ne 0$, injectivity gives $A_1B_2C_1^TA_2=0$.  But $A_1,A_2$ are invertible, $B_2$ is invertible by~\eqref{eq:diagonal} applied to $t=2$, and $C_1$ is invertible by~\eqref{eq:diagonal} applied to $t=1$.  A product of invertible matrices cannot be zero.

  This contradiction shows that no length-20 decomposition exists, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Scope and Outlook}\label{sec:outlook}

\subsection{Capacity Bounds at Length 21}

The argument does not exclude length-21 decompositions because the relevant
occupation caps increase. The following comparison lists the three cap
families used at length~20. Here $L$ denotes the applicable certified
quotient lower bound, including the improvements over $L_0$.
\[
  \begin{array}{c|cc}
    \text{Subspace family} & n=20\text{ cap} & n=21\text{ cap} \\ \hline
    L=19\text{ lines} & 1 & 2 \\
    L=19\text{ all-high 2-planes} & 1 & 2 \\
    L=17\text{ affine hyperplanes} & 3 & 4
  \end{array}
\]
At length~21, the line cap rises to~2, allowing repeated $A$-directions.  The all-high-plane cap also rises to~2, so two high-rank factors may share a plane without contradiction, and the pairwise-rank-one-difference conclusion no longer follows.  Moreover, the flattening inequality $\sum_{t=1}^{21}\rk(A_t)\ge 27$ no longer forces the rank sum to equal~27: the rank-sum excess $\Delta_A=\sum_t\rk(A_t)-27$ need only be $\ge 0$, not zero.  Without zero excess, the saturated identities~\eqref{eq:idempotent} do not hold and the algebraic contradiction dissolves.

The target-19 occupation witnesses and projected rank-23 controls test different aspects of consistency. Neither supplies a complete rank-19 quotient decomposition, establishes its existence, or replaces the forward encoding argument. They are useful diagnostics, not premises of the lower-bound proof.

\subsection{Constraints on Rank-22 Decompositions}

Although the main theorem does not settle rank~22, the saturation analysis gives structural information.  A hypothetical rank-22 decomposition with $\sum_t\rk(A_t)=27$ (zero excess in the $A$-slot) would satisfy all the identities of Lemma~\ref{lem:saturation}.  The product identity~\eqref{eq:product} then implies that at most one $A$-factor can be invertible, since two invertible factors lead to the same zero-product contradiction.  This eliminates the $A$-slot rank-distribution profile $(19,1,2)$ (nineteen rank-1, one rank-2, two rank-3 factors) at zero excess, leaving the profiles $(17,5,0)$ and $(18,3,1)$ as the only possibilities for a zero-excess rank-22 decomposition in that slot.  Under the tensor convention~\eqref{eq:tensor}, the cyclic map $(A,B,C)\mapsto(B,C^T,A^T)$ is a tensor symmetry---$\tr(B\cdot C^T\cdot A)=\tr(ABC^T)$ by cyclic trace---and transpose preserves rank.  The $B$- and $C$-slot split flattenings are also $27\times 27$ permutation matrices, so the saturation analysis transfers verbatim to each slot.  Zero excess in one slot does not by itself imply zero excess in the others.

At positive excess, rank subadditivity no longer supplies these identities. A stronger occupation argument or a different structural constraint might still advance the problem; the present proof does not exclude such extensions.

\section{Machine-Verified Inputs}\label{sec:machine}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} uses a certified quotient table and eight additional
finite exclusions. This section explains how the mathematical premises
are encoded and checked. From the repository root, \texttt{make verify-full}
reconstructs the eight formulas, checks their proofs, independently expands
the quotient table, and reruns its source verifier. The dependency and
command guide is \qpath{reproducibility/README.md}.

\subsection{Wang's Certified Quotient Lower Bounds}

The inherited certificate accompanies Wang's
arXiv:2603.07280v6~\cite{wangautomated}. Replaying its verifier confirms
$R_{\FF}(\Tmmm)\ge20$ from 496 representative subspaces.
Separately, a C++ breadth-first expansion reconstructs the complete
$8{,}283{,}458$-entry lookup table. It:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item parses the certificate's textproto records;
  \item verifies that each of the 13~elementary generators (six left transvections, six right transvections, and $A$-transpose) preserves the tensor under the convention~\eqref{eq:tensor};
  \item computes RREF keys for each expanded subspace;
  \item checks for value conflicts across overlapping orbits.
\end{enumerate}
The re-expanded table has zero conflicts, matches the Gaussian-binomial
dimension counts, and agrees value-by-value with the stored table.

\subsection{Eight Two-Dimensional DRAT Proofs}

For each direction $q$, the strongest singleton row supplies a cap $c_q$. The primary encoder uses $c_q$ distinct Boolean copies with $x_q=\sum_{j=1}^{c_q}y_{q,j}$. Seven orbits have only caps 0 or 1; orbit~489 has one cap of~2. Row sums become cardinality constraints, and total weight~18 becomes matching upper and lower bounds.

To check the direction needed by the proof, take any legal integer vector. Set exactly $x_q$ copies for each $q$ to true. Every row then has precisely its intended integer sum. Each satisfied cardinality constraint extends to a satisfying sequential-counter assignment; a single variable pool allocates disjoint auxiliary variables for all counters, so these extensions coexist. Deduplicating identical rows or omitting a tautology cannot remove this assignment. Conversely, summing the copies of a satisfying assignment recovers legal counts. Thus the CNF is equisatisfiable with the integer system, though the integer system is only necessary for a tensor decomposition. These unrestricted copies differ from the monotone threshold variables used by the separate unary-count implementation.

CaDiCaL solves each instance and produces a DRAT proof, which \texttt{drat-trim} verifies.  The eight proof files range from 4.4\,MiB (orbit~489) to 33.8\,MiB (orbit~487); see Table~\ref{tab:dim2} for per-orbit sizes.  The complete verification pipeline, with source and bundled proof files in \qpath{proof/}, is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An independent semantic generator reconstructs each node-local system directly from the Wang LUT, producing $29{,}210$ rows per orbit.
  \item The CNF encoder converts each system into DIMACS format using the Boolean-copy representation above and sequential-counter clauses for the cardinality constraints.
  \item CaDiCaL returns UNSAT with a DRAT proof.
  \item A separate reconstruction regenerates each CNF exactly and
  \texttt{drat-trim} checks the corresponding refutation.
\end{enumerate}
All eight regenerated CNFs match the stored CNFs, and all eight DRAT replays succeed.

\subsection{Cross-Checks Through Separate Implementations}

A separate verification path within the same research program reconstructed
the finite premises and the algebraic argument:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Re-ran the Wang verifier on the pinned certificate.
  \item Verified 56,448 tensor actions under the symmetry generators.
  \item Checked quotient semantics for 18,704 proof-used subspaces, linking dimension-two rows, line instances, and affine-plane instances to certificate nodes with zero failures.
  \item Replayed the regenerated-CNF DRAT bundle with all checks passing.
  \item Produced an independent family of ``transparent unary-count'' CNF/DRAT certificates for all eight orbits, with a forward-lifting verifier confirming that each legal integer occupation vector maps to the intended CNF assignment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{From Finite Verification to the Tensor Theorem}

The required logical chain is one-way:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-proof-implication}
 R(T_{W_i})\le18
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{legal target-18 occupation counts}
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{satisfiable CNF}.
\end{equation}
The first implication follows from quotienting and padding counts; the
second follows from the Boolean-copy construction above. A checked DRAT
refutation therefore proves $R(T_{W_i})\ge19$, strengthening the inherited
bound $L_0(W_i)=18$. The assignment map is essential: it connects the
formula to every hypothetical short quotient decomposition.

The final structural argument consumes the global bound~20, line
bounds~19, all-high-plane bounds~19, and fourteen affine-plane bounds~17.
With these finite premises established,
Sections~\ref{sec:flattening}--\ref{sec:saturation} give the remaining
deductions as a written proof. Computation supplies the finite
restrictions; rank additivity and the product identity explain why they
are incompatible with a 20-term decomposition.

\section{Conclusions}

Qiushi Engine's research yielded a structural proof of
$R_{\FF}(\Tmmm)\ge21$.
Together with the known upper bound, the interval is $[21,23]$.  The proof introduces a mechanism in which Wang's occupation lattice and split-flattening rank additivity are complementary: the occupation bounds force the rank profile into a zero-excess endpoint, and the saturation identity at zero excess converts the $A$-side counting bound into a $B/C$ algebraic contradiction.  This mechanism works at length~20 because the line cap, all-high-plane cap, and affine-hyperplane cap are exactly $(1,1,3)$, while at length~21 they widen to $(2,2,4)$.

The exact bilinear complexity remains open. Any rank-22 decomposition that has zero excess in a given slot has at most one invertible factor in that slot. The present cap families do not force saturation at length~21 and therefore do not prove a lower bound of~22. Stronger occupation bounds, extensions of the saturation argument, and different structural methods remain possible research directions.

Qiushi Engine's autonomous mathematical research also produced a record of how
this mechanism was found. The integrated Meta-Trace and thematic research
materials connect proposed representations to experiments, corrections,
and subsequent decisions. They show how the difficulty of completing
factor supports led to a question about the full tensor, where an equality
could supply the missing cross-factor information. The report thus offers
both a structural proof and an evidence-linked case study of the
development of mathematical understanding through sustained autonomous
research.

\begin{itemize}
